* Problem Recap /(~45s)/

Iowa grows about 2.5 billion bushels of corn a year — a commanding share of U.S. production. But
Iowa's soil can't support that naturally; it takes heavy nitrogen fertilizer and manure, and much of
that nitrogen leaches into streams as *nitrate*, which is *linked to elevated cancer risk* and, in
infants, to "blue-baby syndrome." Iowa's water often exceeds *10 mg/L, the federal limit*, and
research links levels above just *3 mg/L* to higher cancer risk. Not coincidentally, Iowa is *one of
only a couple of states where cancer rates are still climbing.*

Sensors track nitrate in real time, but coverage is spotty — expensive, breakable, and recently cut
by budget decisions. That leaves water utilities, regulators, and farmers flying blind at the exact
locations they most need information. Our unit of analysis is a *sensor site-day*, and our KPI is:
/can we flag violation risk without a physical sensor there?/

* Data Story /(~40s)/

We started with 162 sensors, filtered to the *85 best*, spanning 4–15 years of records from
2008–2025 — about *160,000 sensor site-days*. For each site we computed its drainage basin —
*verified three independent ways* — then layered on satellite crop data, modeled soil-nitrogen
surplus, and daily weather, all stitched onto a common grid.

Key EDA insight: nitrate has strong *memory* (a persistence baseline beat linear regression), clear
*seasonality* beyond what rain explains, and huge variation by location — some sites average *over
30 mg/L*. Main limitation: sensor coverage is sparse, and annual crop/soil data misses within-season
change.

* Feature Engineering Story /(~40s)/

We kept crop mix, recent rainfall, and — surprisingly — the sensor's raw *location*, which turned out
to be one of the strongest predictors, likely proxying for unmeasured factors like nearby livestock
operations. We engineered *flow-distance and basin-area features per grid cell* rather than using raw
basin-wide weather, since proximity to the sensor mattered more than basin averages. And we dropped
simple annual totals in favor of features that captured *lag* — rain from weeks ago mattered more
than same-day rain, consistent with nitrate's slow movement through soil and tile drains.

* Modeling Journey /(~40s)/

We started with a persistence baseline, which beat basic linear regression outright — though
persistence needs a /local/ sensor, which is exactly why the ungauged case is the hard problem. We
moved to *XGBoost*, since the weather–fertilizer–nitrate relationship is highly nonlinear and the
data is messy — trees beat the classical baselines we tried (persistence, linear models, and
exponential smoothing).

Validation was critical: sensors near each other on the same river report correlated values, so a
naive split would leak information. We held out *entire families of overlapping basins* together, so
every score reflects transfer to genuinely /new/ locations rather than memorizing site-to-site
patterns.

* Final Results /(~55s)/

Our validation is deliberately honest — we hold out whole basin families, so these numbers reflect
unseen watersheds.

*Flagging violations* (the core KPI): across ~160,000 site-days the classifier reaches an *AUC of
0.82* and a *PR-AUC of 0.63 against a 26% base rate — a 2.4× lift over chance*. Tuned to prioritize
catching violations, it flags about *86% of true violation days*; at that setting roughly *60% of its
alarms are false*, and a single recall-vs-precision knob dials that tradeoff. Within any given basin
its *per-site AUC is 0.90* — it's excellent at telling you /when/ nitrate will spike. The
probabilities are also meaningfully calibrated (*Brier 0.137* vs a 0.19 base-rate floor).

*Predicting exact concentration* is harder, as expected: on unseen basins the regressor explains
*about a third of the daily variance (R² ≈ 0.33), with typical error near 4 mg/L*. The decomposition
is the real story — it's twice as good at tracking /movement within/ a basin (within-site R² ≈ 0.42)
as at ranking the /absolute level/ of a brand-new basin (between-site R² ≈ 0.21). So it's a strong
"when and roughly how bad," not a calibrated meter.

The model's behavior is sensible: land near the sensor matters more than distant basin area, rain lag
matters more than same-day rain, and there's a clear spring/early-summer spike. Both gain and
permutation importance agree on the backbone — the *statewide nitrate level that day*, the sensor's
*location and distance geometry*, and *near-field corn* — and permutation confirms the model leans on
what's /immediately upstream/, exactly the riparian effect we engineered for.

* Limitations & Next Steps /(~35s)/

The model may fail at genuinely novel sites far from anything in training, since nitrate is so
location-dependent. Our 85 sites, while curated, don't capture Iowa's full diversity, and annual data
misses within-season shifts.

The most promising addition is *point-source pollution data* — manure runoff from Iowa's livestock
operations, which our location features are likely proxying for now. Soil composition, fertilizer
timing, and tile-drainage data could also help. On modeling, we'd like to try *graph-based
architectures* that explicitly capture the river network's physical connectivity.

* Business / Decision Relevance /(~30s)/

Nitrate predictions let three groups act sooner: *utilities* can start treatment before a spike hits,
*agencies* can target scarce monitoring dollars toward high-risk unmonitored areas, and *farmers* can
time fertilizer application around forecasted rain. It won't replace sensors for regulatory
compliance — but where budget cuts mean the alternative is /no information at all/, even an imperfect
prediction is a major upgrade.
