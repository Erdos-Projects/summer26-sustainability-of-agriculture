# Executive Summary

**Project members:** Erin Bevilacqua, Xiaoying He, Rajpreet Kaur, Jay Lee, Isaac Martin

**Github Repo:** <https://github.com/Erdos-Projects/summer26-sustainability-of-agriculture>

**Project Objective:** Iowa grows 2.5 billion bushels of corn a year, producing enough claories to feed the entire United States. Reaching this scale of production requires heavy nitrogen fertilizer application, and not all of this nitrogen stays in the soil. Excess nitrogen leaches into streams and rivers as nitrate, a Group 2A carcinogen. Iowa's drinking water frequently exceeds the federal limit of 10 mg/L, well above the typical background concentration of 1-2 mg/L, and (more worryingly) the 3 mg/L level recently found to begin being associated with elevated cancer risk. Iowa is the _only_ U.S. state with a rising cancer rate, _a trend that has held since 2015_. Real-time nitrate sensors exist but coverage is sparse, expensive to maintain, and often shrinking due to budget cuts. This leaves water utilities, regulators, and farmers flying blind at most locations. This project asks: **Can we predict nitrate concentration and violation risk at a specific location in Iowa using ONLY publicly available weather and land-use data, without a physical sensor present?**

**Data preparation and computing workflow:** 
The project started with 162 candidate nitrate sensors across Iowa's waterways maintained at both state and federal levels. This was filtered down to 85 sites with sufficient data quality, spanning 4-15 years of records between 2008 and 2025. For each remaining sensor, a drainage basin (the land area which flows into that sensor) was computed and manually validated across three different delineation methods. Three data layers were stiched onto each basin on a common spatial grid: satellite-derived annual crop distribution, annual soil nitrogen surplus, and daily weather data (percipitation, temperature, solar radiation, and related variables). 

Assembling this data and feature engineering was a substantial share of this project's effort. Exploration showed that land near a sensor matters more than land far away from a sensor, so the team aggregated data into distance "buckets" rather than use flat basin-wide averages, and further weighted values by their specific to sensor distance.  Some static geographic features (latitude, longitude, basin area, mean distance to sensor) proved surprisingly important, likely acting as proxies for unmeasured local factors such as nearby livestock operations. We also found through exploration that nitrate violations are highly seasonal, beyond what can be explained by seasonal weather changes, and both sine/cosine seasonality terms improved our models. The team also engineered a leakage-safe cross-site nitrate signal by taking the average nitrate level across all other sites excluding the target site. Some features that were tried and dropped included rolling percipitation features and broadcasting the stale 2017 nitrogen-surplus values forward. Ultimately, our final feature list included weather data lagged by distance to sensor, annual crop data, annual nitrogen soil surplus (kg/acre), geographical features, calendar day information, and cross-site nitrate. This resulted in 68 feature columns total.

**Statistical Validation and KPIs:** 
Model selection followed a deliberate path. Traditional time-series forecasting (e.g., Holt-Winters exponential smoothing) was set aside early, since it fundamentally depends on a site's own historical values, which by definition don't exist at an unmonitored "virtual" site. Modeling individual sites in isolation was tested next and also discarded: even gradient boosting and random forest models trained on a single site underperformed a simple dummy mean baseline (all models scored negative test R²), since no single site has enough data or rich enough features on its own. This confirmed the project's focus should be pooled, cross-site modeling of virtual sensor locations rather than per-site forecasting.

Pooling data across all sites and switching to XGBoost, even untuned and using only basic pre-engineering features, produced the project's first genuinely encouraging signal: 0.24 R² for regression and 0.51 PR-AUC for classification on unseen sites. These became the working baselines that subsequent feature engineering and tuning were measured against.

Validation used Leave-One-Family-Out (LOFO) cross-validation, holding out sensors with overlapping drainage basins together on the same side of the train/test split to prevent spatial data leakage, compared against a Leave-One-Site-Out (LOSO) baseline that ignores basin overlap. LOFO and LOSO scores ended up close, evidence that cross-site leakage wasn't a major practical issue, but LOFO was retained as the honest, conservative number throughout.

Final results on the full, tuned pipeline:

- **Classification (violation flagging):** ROC-AUC 0.82 (ranks violation vs. safe days well), PR-AUC 0.63 against a 26% base violation rate (2.4x better than chance at flagging violations). Per-site AUC of 0.90 shows the model is excellent at detecting when a given basin is spiking. Brier score of 0.137, versus a 0.19 base-rate floor, indicates the model's probabilities are genuinely trustworthy and calibrated. When tuned toward recall, the model can catch the large majority of true violation days at the cost of a higher false-alarm rate, with a single tunable knob to trade recall against precision depending on the use case.
- **Regression (concentration prediction):** LOFO R² ≈ 0.33 (up substantially from the 0.24 untuned baseline), explaining about a third of daily nitrate variance on unseen basins, with a typical error near 4 mg/L. The within-basin R² (≈0.42) is roughly twice the between-basin R² (≈0.21): the model is much better at tracking day-to-day movement within a basin than at ranking the absolute nitrate level of a brand-new basin. In short, it is a strong "when and roughly how bad" tool, not a calibrated concentration meter.

**Statistical tests and deployment implications:** 
An F-test confirmed that nitrate's seasonality holds even after controlling for the seasonality of rainfall, ruling out the risk that the model was simply learning "predict high nitrate in June." Both gain-based and permutation feature importance independently agree on the model's backbone: the statewide nitrate level on a given day, the sensor's location and distance geometry, and near-field corn coverage. Permutation importance in particular confirms the model leans most heavily on what's immediately upstream of a sensor, consistent with the riparian effect the distance-bucket feature engineering was specifically designed to capture. Together, these checks indicate the model is learning physically sensible structure rather than spurious correlations.

Key limitations to keep in mind: the sensor fleet remains sparse relative to Iowa's full scale, the annual crop and soil snapshots miss within-season change, and predictions for genuinely novel basins unlike anything in the training fleet are extrapolations. Natural next steps include incorporating point-source pollution data (livestock manure, which latitude/longitude may currently be partially proxying for), soil composition and tile drainage data, more granular fertilizer application timing, and graph-based models that explicitly capture river-network connectivity between basins.

These findings have been operationalized into a deployable web widget: a user drops a pin anywhere in Iowa, the tool auto-delineates the drainage basin via USGS NLDI, builds the same 68-feature set the models were trained on, and returns a daily predicted-nitrate curve and violation-probability curve for that location, with no local sensor history required.

**Executive Impact:**
Nitrate predictions let three groups act sooner and more precisely than the current sensor network allows. Water utilities can begin treatment before a spike hits rather than reacting after the fact. Regulatory agencies can target monitoring and remediation dollars toward high-risk unmonitored locations rather than spreading resources evenly. Farmers can time fertilizer application around forecasted rain to reduce runoff. The model is not a substitute for physical sensors in regulatory compliance contexts, but in a state where budget cuts mean the realistic alternative at most locations is no information at all, an imperfect, well-calibrated prediction represents a substantial upgrade in situational awareness.
